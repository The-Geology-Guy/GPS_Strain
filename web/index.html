      How triangle strain works

      GPS (GNSS) stations move a few millimetres per year. If three stations move differently, the ground between them is being stretched, shortened, sheared or rotated. Assuming that deformation is uniform inside the triangle, the three velocities are enough to work out how.

      The model

      With x east and y north (metres from the triangle's centre), each station's velocity is a translation plus a rotation plus a strain rate:

      vE = tx − y ω + x εxx + y εxy

      vN = ty + x ω + x εxy + y εyy

      Three stations give six equations for the six unknowns. The velocity uncertainties weight the solution and are carried through to every result (the ± values, from 20,000 Monte Carlo draws).

      Reading the result

      e1H is the greatest horizontal lengthening rate and e2H the least (negative = shortening), along azimuths S1H and S2H. They are in nanostrain per year: 1 nanostrain/yr is 1 mm of change per 1,000 km each year. Maximum shear is e1H − e2H; area strain is e1H + e2H. Rotation is positive counter-clockwise.

      On the map the strain ellipse shows the shape a circle would take (hugely exaggerated); the strain-rate symbol uses black arrows pointing in for shortening and a white double arrow for lengthening.

      Choosing stations

      Use three neighbouring stations with long records, no interior angle under about 30° and no side over about 100 km (GETSI Unit 6). Skinny or large triangles average over too much.

      About GPS Strain

      GPS Strain

      Triangle strain from GNSS velocities

      The GPS triangle strain method is the work of Vince Cronin (Baylor University) and Phil Resor (Wesleyan University), taught in the SERC/GETSI module GPS, Strain, and Earthquakes. GPS Strain is an open-source Python implementation by Luke Pajer that reproduces Cronin and Resor's published worked example exactly.

      Everything runs in your browser. Station velocities are a weekly copy of the Nevada Geodetic Laboratory's MIDAS solution (North America fixed). Terrain comes from USGS 3DEP and fault traces from the USGS Quaternary Fault and Fold Database. The site covers the United States for now.
